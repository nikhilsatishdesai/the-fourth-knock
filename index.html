01 · The game

Something happened at Cedar House.

The Fourth Knock is a short 2.5D murder mystery set inside Cedar House, a colourful creature guesthouse in the Western Ghats, cut off by the 1999 monsoon.

Aren Vale, a platypus traveller who was only passing through, becomes an accidental investigator when property developer Victor Soren is found dead inside an apparently locked room.

9:08 PM

Victor’s broken watch stopped.

9:14 PM

Everyone downstairs heard four knocks.

The murder has a rational solution. The knocks do not.

02 · The project question

Can AI systems be directed like a multidisciplinary production team, rather than used as isolated generators?

That was the experiment underneath the game.

One coherent creative direction had to survive every discipline: story, mystery design, game mechanics, illustration, 3D, animation, voice, music, sound and implementation. It had to survive a long production with handoffs between models, approaches that failed and deliberate pivots, and still arrive as a finished game someone can play in a browser.

03 · My role

I directed. AI systems executed.

I didn’t approach The Fourth Knock as a solo-programming exercise. I acted as its creative director and AI production director.

I defined the premise, the mystery, the characters, the gameplay, the visual direction and the quality bar. Then I directed AI systems through implementation, critique, iteration and final delivery: writing the briefs, reviewing each milestone, rejecting what didn’t serve the game, and deciding what shipped.

04 · Creative north star

Goofy noir on the outside. Proper mystery underneath.

The world could be colourful, charming, funny and stylised, while the murder stayed serious. So noir never meant “make everything dark”. It came from framing, rain, lighting, negative space, typography, sound and the language of detection.

Cozy enough to enter, noir enough to distrust.

Art direction

Warm shelter. Cold rain.

Every frame is built on one contrast: amber light, dark timber and warm textiles inside, cool blue monsoon light pressing at the glass. The rain stays outside, even in interior shots, so the house always reads as shelter.

05 · Gameplay

The player actually solves something.

This isn’t a walking simulator or a visual novel. You collect words, build deductions, bring them back to the people they contradict, and the case only moves when the logic holds.

  1. Observe
  2. Inspect
  3. Question
  4. Collect clue words
  5. Form deduction
  6. Re-question
  7. Resolve contradiction

  1. Observe

    Walk Cedar House in real time. Anything worth a closer look is marked in the world.

  2. Inspect

    Evidence opens as an illustrated panel with details to examine.

  3. Question

    Choose what to ask. Everyone answers in character, with a voice.

  4. Collect clue words

    What you notice becomes words in your case file.

  5. Form deduction

    Place clue words into a Case Note and test whether it holds.

  6. Re-question

    Bring evidence back to the person it contradicts.

  7. Resolve contradiction

    When the logic holds, the story moves forward.

06 · Three pivots

Where the impressive option lost.

Three decisions shaped the game more than any single asset. In each one, the more technically impressive option was the wrong one.

Pivot 1

Full 3D → 2.5D

The first direction was fully 3D: modelled characters in a modelled lounge. On paper it was the more ambitious option. In practice it was harder to keep consistent, more expensive per character and pose, less distinctive, and less suited to a short mystery that lives on faces and reactions.

I stopped it at the visual gate and redirected the whole look. The characters became simple illustrated cutouts, acted through expressive poses, inside a stylised 3D world. That constraint became the game’s identity.

Technical ambition is not the same as creative quality.

Pivot 2

Over-engineering the locked room

Rejected · simulate the trick

Still failed its own accuracy check after 0.474 simulated seconds.

Shipped · an ordinary spring night latch
  1. Release the hold-back
  2. Pull the door shut
  3. The spring tongue catches

The locked room originally depended on a lace-and-bolt trick: a cord looped round a sliding bolt and pulled from under the door. The implementation responded by building a physics simulation of it, with hundreds of cord segments, friction models and force calculations. That was a lot of machinery for something the player only needs to understand.

I rejected it. The final mechanism is an ordinary spring night latch with a hold-back catch. It is deterministic, readable, and demonstrated in three steps the player performs.

Use the cheapest reliable system that delivers the intended player experience.

Pivot 3

Sets → one real building

The earlier environment problem wasn’t asset quality. It was spatial coherence: each room felt like a separate set. So the house was planned as one building before any room was touched.

The veranda facade is the lounge’s west wall, with the same windows and the same door. The stair climbs through the lounge’s north wall into the corridor above. Every door hangs on real hinges and opens the same way from both sides, and leaving a room means walking through it.

Once the building made architectural sense, the atmosphere improved on its own.

07 · The opening, four times

Four openings. One target.

The 40-second opening (an unsigned letter, a night train, a ghat road, a lit house) was rebuilt several times. These four versions show the production method changing while the target held.

V13D cinematic

  • Technically polished.
  • The wrong medium for an illustrated cast.

V2Code-drawn 2D

  • The right medium.
  • It felt programmer-generated.

V3ComfyUI painted + animated

  • Painterly atmosphere and real motion.
  • Not every generated movement was equally strong.

FinalHybrid

  • The painted shots, the code-drawn ghat road, and the 3D move into play.

In V3, each painting was guided by the code-drawn layout of its own shot (Z-Image-Turbo with ControlNet), then brought to life with Wan 2.2 image-to-video. Aren himself is always the approved drawings, never regenerated.

The production method was allowed to change. The creative target was not.

Audio was a production, not a layer.

The Four Knocks

Three strikes. A longer pause. A fourth.

The knocks are both a clue and the game’s sonic identity. Their rhythm is a contract, fixed wherever it appears: in the game, its captions, the letter’s underline, the trailer and the title.

How they were built
  • Each strike layers a generated knuckle-on-door transient, a floor thud for weight, and synthesised wood resonances.
  • At 9:14 they’re heard from the lounge, through the ceiling and down the stairwell. At dawn it’s the same strikes, heard through Victor’s door.
  • Strikes land at 0.15, 1.10, 2.05 and 4.30 seconds. The fourth is slightly firmer.
  • The mix was rebalanced so the wood still reads on laptop speakers.

10 · Quality control

“I didn’t knock.” “I did knock.”

16 of 18 generated takes were heard as the opposite.

Convincing isn’t the same as correct.

One line near the end of the story rests on a single syllable. The voice model kept swallowing the “n’t”, and speech recognition heard 16 of 18 takes as “I did knock”: the opposite meaning, in the line where meaning matters most. The automatic selection would have shipped the reversal.

So the pipeline gained a polarity test. Every line whose meaning rests on a negation (19 of them) is checked against its flipped twin, and a take heard reversed can’t be chosen.

AI output can’t be accepted because it sounds convincing. It has to preserve intent.

11 · Final polish

By the end, the project didn’t need more ideas. It needed fewer visible mistakes.

The last pass added no features. It hunted visible defects:

  • Head clipping
  • Sprite consistency
  • Furniture intersections
  • Collision
  • Depth & occlusion
  • Z-fighting
  • Door traversal
  • Staging
  • Eyelines
  • UI clipping
  • Audio start-up

12 · Evolution

It didn’t come from one prompt.

  1. Concept

    The story, the rational murder, the unexplained knocks, the cast.

  2. Full 3D direction

    Modelled characters in a modelled lounge. Stopped at the visual gate.

  3. 2.5D pivot

    Illustrated cutouts inside a stylised 3D house.

  4. Browser-first architecture

    Plain JavaScript modules and Three.js. No build step; it runs in any modern desktop browser.

  5. Cedar House rebuild

    One building, walked passages, doors that open both ways.

  6. Hybrid animated opening

    Four production methods, one target.

  7. Full audio production

    Cast, score, rooms, the knocks, and a QA pipeline.

  8. Final director’s polish

    No new features. Fewer visible mistakes.

  9. v1.0

    Frozen, archived and playable. September 2026.

14 · What I learned

Four lessons

  1. Taste matters more when execution gets cheaper.

    Fast generation raises the value of deciding what deserves to survive.

  2. Constraints improve AI production.

    The game got stronger when it gave up the more technically ambitious 3D direction.

  3. Models need direction, not just prompts.

    The strongest work came from specifying intent and constraints, while leaving implementation free.

  4. Finishing is not generating.

    Final quality came from consistency, continuity, readability, pacing, testing and defect removal.

Reflection

Working creatively with AI felt less like operating a generator and more like directing an unusually fast multidisciplinary production team.

The systems can produce code, images, environments, animation and sound. They don’t decide which version deserves to survive. Throughout production one question stayed the same:

Does this serve the experience?

When the answer was no, the work changed, however technically impressive it was.
